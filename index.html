Research

I work on whether machine-learning models built on brain MRI hold up when they meet data from a different scanner, hospital or study — and on what to measure when they do not.

My current project evaluates Alzheimer's disease classification across ADNI, OASIS-3 and AIBL, testing transfer in both directions. Two results came out of it. A preprocessing mismatch between cohorts turned out to be able to manufacture findings that look methodological: in our pipeline it simultaneously produced an apparent benefit from feature harmonization and an apparent advantage for a pre-trained brain foundation model, and correcting it removed both. Separately, harmonization does reliably remove the site signal it targets — cross-cohort site decodability falls from about 0.82 to 0.59 — while disease classification does not improve. The metric normally used to declare harmonization successful can therefore diverge from the objective it stands for.

More broadly I am interested in evaluation itself: confounds that survive preprocessing, calibration under distribution shift, and the gap between a model that scores well and a model that can be trusted somewhere new.

Methods I work with

3D convolutional encoders in PyTorch; ANTs affine registration to MNI and HD-BET skull stripping; ComBat, DeepComBat and adversarial gradient-reversal harmonization; leave-one-cohort-out designs; subject-clustered bootstrap and paired DeLong testing; calibration by Brier score and precision–recall at low prevalence.

I also spend a lot of time on quality control — intensity and registration parity between cohorts, per-volume outlier detection, and gates that state on every verdict which axes they checked and which they did not. Most of what I have learned about generalization came from that part rather than from the modelling.
